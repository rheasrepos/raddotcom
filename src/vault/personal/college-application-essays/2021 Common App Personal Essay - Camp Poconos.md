*Prompt 5 — Discuss an accomplishment, event, or realization that sparked a period of personal growth and a new understanding of yourself or others. (650 words)*

Growing up, I was shamed for eating out with friends only to be force-fed highly caloric Indian food at home each night. Food is my parent's love language, yet their language surrounding food is restrictive and obsessive. My parents blame each other for my chubby physique, inadvertently criticizing me for the way I exist in the world.

Spring 2018, I was already working with a trainer my parents hired and navigating fitness apps my mother sent me, so I didn't understand why my dad texted me a link to a "weight loss camp" in Philadelphia. I wanted the traditional sleepaway camp experience I was never allowed, so I decided I'd go, for myself, not for my parents.

My first year at Camp Poconos Trails was freeing. The toxicity surrounding body image at home trapped me for 14 years, but here communication felt refreshing, genuine. Walking up a nearly 60-degree hill we colloquially called "cardiac," or doing Zumba to "Kiss Kiss," you heard people freely panting instead of concealing their exhaustion for fear of embarrassment. We could spill out life stories at lights off or on 3-minute walks to zipline.

I went back a second year and kept close contact with my camp friends. Two years later, I was invited back as a counselor. Being a former fat camp attendee, I thought I'd gotten good at reading campers, but my acquired talents felt worthless. Lower camp was riddled with young average-sized girls who believed their chubby cheeks and archaically-computed BMI spoke more truth about their bodies than their feelings did.

I patrolled the cabin for jokes that could trigger girls' past trauma that were explained in parent questionnaires. I assured girls they looked amazing in dresses they felt scared to wear at home, and had daily porch talks with campers who wanted their phone, to leave, or comfort during panic attacks, something I was only trained by my own experience to deal with. As a sort of in-house therapist, carried their worries with me every day, aiming to be sensitive to their pasts while making sure I didn't treat them too differently, making them feel othered.

Sometimes, help was beyond my control; Charlotte was so homesick, that camp brought back her stress-induced alopecia. Walking to water aerobics, she explained she "hated being here" but leaving would waste her parents' money. It took courage to share the troubles plaguing her, but as she did, her breath slowed and eyes dried.

As my campers started to realize their need for communication, I was keeping my own secret. For the past four years, I'd told my friends that I'd gone to the sports camp down the road from Poconos Trails, filtered my speech when I got home to not seem different, and feared questioning. While I wanted to share pictures of my memories, I worried they'd wonder why everyone was overweight, that if people saw me there, I'd validate my own fatness. Especially as a counselor, it was hard to hide why summer camp made me so tired and emotionally drained while on calls with friends.

On a weekly facetime call with my best friend, I decided I couldn't keep lying. I told her about Pocono Trails and even introduced her to some of my campers. She was more shocked about my apprehension than about the camp itself; I had nothing to worry about. After flying back home, I found myself truthfully answering "What happened at camp?" when exploring a local thrift store and catching up over Thai food with my two closest friends.

I like feeling open and unconstricted. Being vulnerable feels risky yet exciting. Even if the constriction of home has hindered my ability to embrace all parts of myself, I've committed myself to finding spaces where I can let all of me reign free: on calls with friends, on our scenic drives up the Mulholland overlook, in Party City parking lots.
