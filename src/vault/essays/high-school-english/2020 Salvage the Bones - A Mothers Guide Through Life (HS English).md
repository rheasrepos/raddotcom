# A Mother's Guide Through Life — Salvage the Bones essay
*Rhea Madhogarhia (HS English, 10th grade ~2020, recovered from Google Drive).*

> See now that I, even I am he, and there is no god with me; I kill and I make alive, I wound and I heal, neither is there any can deliver out of my hand. — Deuteronomy

These words from the Bible can be interpreted as describing the all powerful force and nature of a higher being like God, but in Salvage the Bones Jesmyn Ward seems to interpret this higher power as a force of nature. The verse speaks to the fact that no one is immune from the destruction that a higher power can bring and that the effects of it's wreckage is inescapable. It speaks to the uncontrollable impact that a strong force, such as nature, or even God, can have on others. Throughout the book we see Esch's connection to her surroundings and the impact that nature and environment has on her feelings. When thinking about nature, we often associate it with the persona of a mother, a strong and loving being. In the book, motherhood becomes a prominent theme as we follow and understand the story of Esch, a newly pregnant child, China, a mother of puppies, and the memory of Mama. As the memory of Mama often haunts and lingers around Esch's mind, we see that even though she is dead, she is very much alive in the spirit of her family's lives. Just as Mother Nature brought destruction to Esch's family and forced them to set on a new path and the loss of China deeply hurt and broke down Skeetah, Mama's death brought a similar kind of tragedy and loss that created her legacy and stayed with them for their lives without her. As Esch sees the effect Hurricane Katrina had on everyone around her, she comes to understand the strength and power of a mother and the impact a mother has on her child.

Esch once ignored the reality of her being the mother, but as she saw the impact that motherhood had on those a mother protected, she realized the responsibility she must bear in order to create a legacy that a mother should. Esch now realizes she has a responsibility to her family, which she realizes is not just the responsibility of love, but also to expose them to harsh realities of life. After the Hurricane, Skeetah had stayed to look for his companion, China, and Esch finds the need to share the story of the wreckage with him when they reunite: "Like Skeetah told me the story of the last thing that Mama said to us, I will tell him this. This was a liquor bottle, I will say. And this, this was a window. This, a building" (254). Once again, Esch recalls the memory of her mother's words signifying the impact her late mother has on her to this day. As she realizes how impactful her mother's last words to her were, she finds it important to bring through the legacy of other mothers, in this case, Mother Nature. There is a parallel between the wreck that Mama left behind for her family and the wreck that Mother Nature created; as Mama's death left an imprint on the family, it helped the family grow and live without her. As Esch's family has become familiar with this destruction, Esch realizes that to move on she must take on the responsibility of carrying on the legacy of a tragedy. The details of the physical wreckage suggest Esch's attention to details is allowing her to move on but remember the legacy and image of loss in her mind. It's a reminder that even the smallest things, every child of Mother Nature, felt the shock and burden of her rage. She wants to share the reminder that loss is inevitable. As Esch internalizes the scene of her destroyed town she realizes that she must share the legacy of Katrina with her child: "I will tie the glass and stone with string, hang the shards above my bed, so that they will flash in the dark and tell the story of Katrina, the mother that swept into the Gulf and slaughtered" (255). The shards from the wreck that Esch intends to keep for her child express her internalization that legacies should last generations. In a way it suggests that a mother's impact is timeless and inescapable, just as the Hurricane and Mama's death were out of Esch's control. The personification of the hurricane as a mother indicates that Esch did in fact see Mother Nature as acting out on her world of children. Because of a mother's responsibility and control, the wrecking of the gulf was a choice of the Hurricane. This reflects the hard choices a mother must make, even if they cause inexpressible pain to those after them. Esch finds that strength in motherhood has consequences, but is necessary for those affected to move on and grow.

After Esch understands the responsibility that a mother carries, she comes to find out that the weight that a mother's presence carries should allow her children to survive and rebuild themselves even after they're gone. The strength a mother has, lets her children find strength in themselves to take on life themselves and not run away from those things that will inevitably affect us. After describing her desire to share the legacy of the Hurricane with her own child, Esch describes Hurricane Katrina in a more vicious way, "She was the murderous mother who cut us to the bone but left us alive, left us naked and bewildered as wrinkled newborn babies, as blind puppies, as sun-starved newly hatched baby snakes" (255). Esch's comparison of the hurricane to a vicious mother suggests that the strength of a mother can be used in many ways and the kind of destruction she can bring is far greater than most realize. The image of barely surviving shows that as a mother leaves you with loss, she is giving you space to build yourself into something, like a renewal of themselves. A mother's job is not to completely destroy, it is to create a disturbance so that people must find a way out themselves, so that they can be prepared to know how to survive when more loss comes their way. This is similar to how Esch's family finds the loss of their house and possessions a bit familiar since they've already been through the loss of their most prized possession, Mama. This comparison continues as Esch continues describing the hurricane as a mother: "She left us to learn to crawl. She left us to salvage. Katrina is the mother we will remember until the next mother with large, merciless hands, committed to blood, comes" (255). Esch describes the hurricane as abandoning her and her family and leaving them to learn how to grow up themselves. In a way this parallels the way Junior had to grow up by himself, as he created the strength to learn how to grow up without a mother. The legacy of Katrina will live with them until the next destruction comes because they find that it is a mother's impact that guides them through every stage of their life. Before the hurricane it was Mama's death and for Esch's journey into motherhood, it will be the hurricane.

This novel is neither happy nor tragic because it shows both the love that mothers bring but also the harsh reality of their role in their children's lives.
