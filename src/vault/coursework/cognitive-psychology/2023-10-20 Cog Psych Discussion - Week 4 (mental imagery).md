Rhea Madhogarhia
Week 4
Prof Heald

One seemingly obvious observation that can be made is that the mental representations
we make during perception, that third stage after we receive the distal and proximal stimulus,
are a form of mental imagery. Perceptual experiences, as we talked about in previous chapters,
do not necessarily match an “objective” reality. Instead they are representations that we
construct that are based on our expectations and experiences. If, when talking about visual
percepts, we claim that we are mentally constructing images that may not match the distal
stimulus, then it seems that perception heavily involves mental imagery, or that the two are very
similar. Kosslyn, Ganis, and Thompson’s conclusion in their paper on the “Neural Foundations
of Imagery” supports this idea: imagery has the ability to stand in for perception in some
situations because of its ability to engage our motor system and affect our body. Mental rotation
experiments, conducted by Richter et. al and Parsons et al both showed the motor cortices were
activated when patients were asked to mentally rotate objects or their hands.
Earlier this week, I was puzzled by the case of Patient DF (from our textbook) and her
inability to match an envelope’s direction into a slot while having success “mailing” it. I believe
that mental imagery’s effect on motor function may provide some more context. Patient DF’s
task was to physically rotate a card when the goal was to match directionality - which proved
hard after the brain damage she suffered - but as soon as she was asked to “mail” it, she was
able to match the directionality and mail the card. The process of mailing the card likely required
some mental imagery, maybe imagining past experiences of putting a card in a mailbox. When
that experience was first created, mailing an envelope, our perception was involved: identifying
the mailbox, noticing the size and orientation of the letter, etc.. If perception really rests on
mental representations of objects, then that is a mental image reference for Patient DF to call
upon. Although she may never have been asked directly to match the orientation of a card to a
slot, she has mailed an envelope, something she already has created a mental image of that
had an effect on her motor functions.
Studies that aimed to determine whether mental imagery and perception were affected in
the same way in brain damaged individuals has strengthened the relationship between imagery
and perception. Bissiach and Luzzti used patients suffering from unilateral neglect in their vision
to test whether or not a patient would neglect a certain side of an image when they were
conjuring up a mental image even though their condition in theory only affects one side of their
vision. The patient failed to create the image on the same side that their vision failed, even
though they hypothetically don’t need visual cues to be able to make a mental image. While
there are strong parallels between the processes and physiology behind mental imagery and
perception, the textbook stressed that they are not exactly the same process - dissociations
between imagery and perception and evidence that they each have unique activity in some
brain regions also exist.
