Insight in problem solving has been defined, in the textbook, as the sudden realization or
comprehension of a solution to a problem involving the reorganization of the mental
representation of a problem that reveals an previoulsy unobvious answer. The latter part of this
definition, the unobvious answer, is something that many of the experiments described in the
textbook involving creativity looked for. It seems as though creativity is an innate part of
insightful problem solving - “aha” moments or findings, a common way to describe an insight,
are often seen as the unusual and creative solution. While creativity is an integral part of insight
and both involve the redirection of attention away from common and usual solutions in problem
solving, not all creative solutions can be categorized as an insight, and can be the result of
conscious and unconscious processes or decisions.
First, to discuss some commonalities between creativity and insight that push us closer
to seeing that insight is creative, we can look at the nine-dot problem, an experiment used in
many insight studies (core reading) and discussed in the textbook as demonstrating “outside the
box” thinking, a key characteristic of creativity. Solving the nine-dot problem has been attributed
both to insight problem solving and creativity whcih tells us that when people are solving the
nine-dot problem, realizing that a usual solution doesn’t work causes them to shift their frame
and representation of the problem to find a more unusual answer. With both creativity and
insight, the attentional frame is shifted so that ordinary solutions are blocked from focus and
more cognitive resources can be allocated to finding the unique and unusual solution. While this
can happen internally for some participants in this study, in lecture we were told that researchers
told users themselves to “think outside the box” which also shifts the attentional frame and
encourages a manipulation of the representation of the problem. The solution to this problem is
not obvious and requires a reorganization of the problem, not seeing the nine dots as a confined
box, characteristics of a problem with a solution that requires creativity and often induces
insight. Another example of creativity and insight working together is the case of Odon and his
baby extracting device. His insight, aided through analogical problem solving, that the way you
extract a cork from a wine bottle could be applied to extracting a stuck baby from a mother was
btoh a creative solution to a medical problem and a creative and unusual mapping/application of
the video he had watched about wine corks. The velcro and burr example showcases
something similar. Overall, insight is a phenomenon that, by it’s nature, produces creative
solutions.
Thinking or problem solving creatively can also be described as a process, and unlike
insight, this process can be conscious and gradual. Baird’s alternate uses task, in which
participants are prompted to find unusual uses for a common object, begs for creative problem
solving and divergent thinking. Finding other uses for an object is not necessarily the result of a
bunch of “aha” moments - someone could very methodically think about what parts of an object
feel and look like and parse out the different uses and interactions that part could have with our
environment, but because the use is not typical or common, we say that unusual solutions are
creative or somewhat original. Open monitoring meditation is also a process by which creativity
is enhanced. The act of paying attention to what comes into the midn and following the thought
is very conscious and deliberate - not only is this different from insight’s unconscious nature, but
also it shows that creativity can be induced and improved.
Knowing the similarities and differences between creativity and insight problem solving
can be helpful, especially if we want to train ourselves to think a certain way. While insight may

be more common for experts, and arguably so given taht Odon was a mechanic and many
insights come to people less impacted by mental set and functional fixedness that can come
with expertise in a certain area, it seems hard to be able to control how good or frequently
someone insightfully problem solves whereas our textbook was able to outline many ways for us
to increase our creativity. Professor Heald mentioned that, in the nine-dot problem, many
participants could still not find the solution even when given a hint. Knowing when insight will
occur (predicting that you will be able to solve a problem insightfully) , though maybe possible to
some degree through mood manipulation, seems very variable and unknown. Our brains may
have different activation patterns before we solve a problem analytically or insightfully but
whether or not we can induce those activation patterns like we can increase the chances of
solving something creatively seems unlikely.
