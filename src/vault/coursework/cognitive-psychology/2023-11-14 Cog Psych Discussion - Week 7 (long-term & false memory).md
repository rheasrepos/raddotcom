Rhea Madhogarhia
Week 7
Long-term memory is commonly known for its seemingly infinite storage capacity.
However, the fragility of this system and its susceptibility to inaccuracies and interference effects
shed light on the personalized and constructed, and therefore sometimes false, nature of
long-term memory. I was especially struck by the idea brought up in lecture that our memories
are constructed, and, as the core reading mentioned quite a few times, our memories are not
comparable to video recordings. While this may seem obvious, viewing memory in a legal
context, it made me wonder if I were testifying on the stand, would I be sure that I was telling the
truth - after all, I created my memories and, therefore, they may be unintentionally biased or
false. What we believe as true may actually just be products of certain encoding effects. For
example, the propaganda effect tells us that people may believe something to be more true
simply because it has been a repeated stimuli. The retrieval process may also alter the way we
respond and present information, and in a legal setting, that may complicate things. Baddely’s
Diving Experiment tells us that the context in which you are retrieving information matters. In a
courtroom you are likely far removed from the context you encoded the information of a crime. I
hypothesize that a prosecutor may ask leading questions in a manner that causes you to
answer a question incorrectly or mix up facts on a timeline.
It is helpful to observe the parallel between memory and perception. The parallel
becomes evident when considering the constructive nature of both processes. Just as
perception involves assembling sensory information into a coherent representation of the world
(stage 3 of visual perceptions is the constructed mental representation - the perceptual object
does not always match the real world object), long-term memory entails retrieving past
experiences (experiences that when first put into memory, were also constructed by our minds).
This retrieval, however, is far from perfect, as evidenced by the susceptibility to modification and
damage in both consolidation and reconsolidation discussed in the textbook. In both perception
and memory, the brain actively interprets and integrates information (just as in the blue basket
experiment with List A and List B by Hupback), opening avenues for errors and distortions. In
memory, interference occurs when new information disrupts the retrieval of existing memories or
when existing memories interfere with the encoding of new information. This bears resemblance
to perceptual interference, or distractions, where external stimuli can distort the interpretation of
sensory input. Understanding these parallels highlights the intertwined nature of cognitive
processes, fostering a holistic view of how the mind constructs and maintains our subjective
experiences.
In conclusion, the fragility of long-term memory, intertwined with its susceptibility to
inaccuracies and modifications, invites a deeper exploration of cognitive processes. By drawing
parallels between memory and perception, we illuminate the common threads that weave
through these fundamental aspects of human cognition. This understanding not only
underscores the importance of a nuanced approach when cognitively modeling long-term
memory, but also allows us to bring a new understanding to treatments of psychological
disorders. More than 13 million people have PTSD in America, and our understanding of
reconsolidation has allowed us to come up with a type of treatment that modifies memory’s

effects on the body. Having and continuing to study the nuances of long term memory seems
significant, especially when considering its possible effects on sentencing in the courtroom.
