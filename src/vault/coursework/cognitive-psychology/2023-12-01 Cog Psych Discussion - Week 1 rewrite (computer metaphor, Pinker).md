Rhea Madhogarhia
Week 1 Discussion Post Re-write
I found that Steven Pinker’s “How the Mind Works” was helpful in diving deep into the
importance of the computer in the cognition paradigm presented in the first chapter of “Cognitive
Psychology: Connecting Mind, Research, And Everyday Experience”. While I was convinced,
while reading the textbook, of the analogy between the computer and the brain, Pinker went
deeper into the similarities comparing our brain to hardware and our mind to software and even
started the chapter discussing the complexities of building a human-like robot. In this
discussion, Pinker goes into great detail about how difficult it would be to program legs instead
of wheels and how complex a machine’s visual system could be, explaining all the
considerations an engineer may have to make. While his discussion of the mind as a well
designed computer system was intriguing and convincing, Pinker’s discussion and
appreciation for the mind’s ability to fill in the gaps, solve incredibly challenging and
distinctly human problems and make inferences made a convincing argument for why it
so challenging to robotically replicate the human mind.
Pinker’s unveiling of the decisions we make and the stages in which information is
processed in our brain, especially our ability to fill in gaps and visually see the world as it exists
unlike a camera or a robot’s visual system’s deficiencies, is lengthy and clearly demonstrates
that we do not just “see” things; our mind constructs them for us. Professor Heald echoed this
point in lecture when discussing Tollman’s experiment as a marker for why we needed the
transition from behaviorism to the cognitive revolution. In Tollman’s experiment, behaviorism,
studying the brain based on only behavior rather than recognizing the presence of more hidden
processes in the mind, wasn’t able to explain why mice knew where the food was in a maze
even when their positioning in the maze was flipped. The mouse’s ability to find the food when
placed in a different orientation means a mental map of the maze was created and, instead of
associating finding food with certain motor steps and turns, the mouse placed itself in it’s mental
map and figured out where to go based on its mental representation of the maze (likely involving
memory, mental rotation, and visual imagery). Pinker points out that these inferences and the
information processing we do coupled with needing to control our limbs and move/behave
accordingly makes replicating the brain and body in robot form even more challenging. He goes
on to say that it is possible to bombard a robotic software with billions of nodes of training data
and facts. While this may make a robot behave similarly to a human, the human is more of an
intelligent system than a hard coded one. It is this distinction that really makes replicating the
human brain a more daunting task.
I do not possess adequate knowledge to refute Pinker’s claims or descriptions of mental
processes, but I am convinced that we take for granted just how much work our minds do in a
split second. It makes me wonder how Watson was ever able to reject the idea that inferences
could be or should be made about the “black box” in between a stimulus and the behavior
created from it. Watson was focused on predictions and controlling behavior but, to me, didn’t
seem that intrigued as to why our minds created that behavior from that stimulus. That “why?”,
which Pinker seems to be determined to answer in his later chapters, has so much importance
for me and I’m grateful that the cognitive revolution has allowed us to continue exploring it.
