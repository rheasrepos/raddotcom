Discussion Question Option 1:
Although the cognitive revolution led us to a time where making inferences about the mind brain
connection became a worthy and accepted area of study, using a neural perspective and neurophysiology
to further our understanding of cognition gives the field of cognitive science more credibility and a basis
for further research to have findings to contest, qualify, or generally grow from. While I believe that we
are exceptional at being able to make connections and infer, just as Watson was skeptical of the merit of
studying cognition, many people may believe that studying cognition without looking at the brain and our
anatomy is just making guesses as to how mental processes occur. Recording synapses and electrical
activity in the brain is currently the closest we can get to making Leibnitz's thought experiment seem a
little less unsettling.
One example where I found myself more convinced of an idea because of the neural perspective
was using McIntosh’s experiment on awareness and prefrontal cortex interactionsDo to reinforce the idea
that we do not perceive the world directly, instead we perceive the world through mental representations
which are built on our experiences. McIntosh’s experiment, in which they presented two different tones
that were associated with visual stimuli, recorded that the group of patients that became aware of the
association between the tone and the visual stimuli were basically classically conditioned to expect the
visual stimuli when the tone was played. The functional connections in their brain were different than
when they started the experiment and the correlations between different neural regions were stronger than
the group that was unaware of the tonal and visual association. The group that noticed the association
used that awareness as the experiment went on and, subsequently, their neural connections reflected that.
In the textbook and in lecture this was referred to as experience-dependent plasticity - as their awareness
built, they began experiencing the study in a new way. They were now predicting something that was
going to happen/perceiving the rest of the study based on their learned experience from the study already.
Experience-dependent plasticity was pretty clearly demonstrated in Blakemore and Cooper’s kitten
experiment in which they raised kittens in an environment where they only saw vertical striped lines on
the walls. When normally raised kittens are exposed to lines in many orientations, their visual cortex’s
contain feature detecting neurons that respond to all experienced orientations. Blakemore and Cooper’s
kittens were found to only have feature detecting neurons for vertically oriented bars. The kittens' neurons
only firing at the vertical lines signaled that they were only perceiving the stimuli that they had been
exposed to growing up.
The idea of “perceived reality” always seems to be controversial in conversation. Plato’s allegory
of the cave, mentioned in lecture, brings up the idea, but some people (including people in my HUM class
last year) are quick to brush the idea off or get angry at the fact that what we are seeing might not be an
objective reality or truth. Personally, I buy in, but I think that experience-dependent plasticity is a concept
that could make perceived reality seem less far-fetched for other people. Looking at it from a neural
perspective and remembering Blakemore and Cooper’s kitten experiment, it doesn’t seem so hard to
believe that if our brain is changed and trained through our experiences, our experiences manipulate our
perception, and our reality is formed by our own perception, then everyone may perceive reality
differently. Understanding that we may all perceive things differently based on how we were raised or our
lived experiences could be important for further psychological research because it could help determine
eligibility requirements for studies with visual components to make sure, especially in control cases of
studies, participants are reacting to similar visual stimuli. It could also help give further insight into how
brain damage will affect people differently and also help us understand more about how people learn or
struggle with learning and storing new information in memory.
